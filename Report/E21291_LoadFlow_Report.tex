\section{Introduction}
Power flow analysis is one of the most fundamental computations in power systems engineering. At its core, it answers a deceptively simple question: given a network of generators, loads, and transmission lines, what are the voltages and power flows at every point in the system? The answer to this question underpins nearly every operational and planning decision made by power utilities, from economic dispatch to contingency screening.

This report documents the development of a load flow solver from first principles in Python, its application to the standard IEEE 9-bus test system, and a subsequent investigation into system response under varying load conditions. The IEEE 9-bus system is a well-established benchmark in power engineering --- it comprises 3 generators, 3 transformer-connected load buses, and 6 transmission lines, all on a 100 MVA base. It is sufficiently small to permit hand verification, yet complex enough to exhibit realistic phenomena such as voltage sensitivity and reactive power coupling.



The notation used throughout this report is defined here for clarity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_i = |V_i| \angle \delta_i$ --- complex bus voltage at bus $i$ (magnitude in pu, angle in degrees)
    \item $Y_{ik} = |Y_{ik}| \angle \theta_{ik}$ --- element of the Y-bus admittance matrix. Note: $\theta_{ik}$ is the angle of the admittance element, distinct from $\delta_i$, the voltage angle. The shorthand $\delta_{ik} = \delta_i - \delta_k$ denotes voltage angle differences.
    \item $P_i, Q_i$ --- active and reactive power injection at bus $i$ (in pu on 100 MVA base)
    \item $N$ --- total number of buses in the system
    \item $\epsilon$ --- convergence tolerance ($10^{-4}$ pu in this work)
    \item $J$ --- the Jacobian matrix, with sub-matrices $J_1$ through $J_4$
\end{itemize}

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{Power Balance Equations}
The formulation begins with Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL). The net current injection at any bus equals the sum of currents flowing through all connected branches. Expressing this in terms of the admittance matrix, the complex power injection at bus $i$ is given by~\cite{saadat}:
\begin{equation}
S_i = P_i + jQ_i = V_i I_i^* = V_i \left( \sum_{k=1}^{N} Y_{ik} V_k \right)^*
\label{eq:complex_power}
\end{equation}
where the asterisk denotes the complex conjugate. Equation~(\ref{eq:complex_power}) constitutes the fundamental power balance relation from which all subsequent derivations follow.

Expressing the voltages in polar form ($V_i = |V_i| \angle \delta_i$) and the admittance as $Y_{ik} = |Y_{ik}| \angle \theta_{ik}$, then expanding the product and separating real and imaginary parts, the active and reactive power injections at bus $i$ become:
\begin{equation}
P_i = \sum_{k=1}^{N} |V_i| |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \cos(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:P_calc}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Q_i = -\sum_{k=1}^{N} |V_i| |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \sin(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:Q_calc}
\end{equation}
These two equations govern the load flow problem. At each bus, the known quantities are either the power injection (for load buses), the voltage magnitude and active power (for generator buses), or both voltage magnitude and angle (for the slack bus). The unknowns consist of the remaining quantities.

\subsection{Mismatch Equations}
The Newton-Raphson method works by iteratively reducing the ``mismatch'' between what the power \textit{should} be (specified by the network data) and what it \textit{actually} is (calculated from the current estimate of voltages). This mismatch is defined as:
\begin{align}
\Delta P_i &= P_{i}^{spec} - P_{i}^{calc} \label{eq:dP} \\
\Delta Q_i &= Q_{i}^{spec} - Q_{i}^{calc} \label{eq:dQ}
\end{align}
At the solution, both $\Delta P_i$ and $\Delta Q_i$ should be zero (or sufficiently close --- the implementation uses a tolerance of $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$~pu, corresponding to approximately 10~kW or 10~kVAr on the 100~MVA base).

The key insight is that Equations~(\ref{eq:dP}) and (\ref{eq:dQ}) form a system of nonlinear equations in the unknowns $\delta_i$ and $|V_i|$. A direct analytical solution is not feasible; an iterative numerical method is required.

\subsection{Newton-Raphson Iterative Scheme}
The Newton-Raphson method operates by linearising the nonlinear mismatch equations around the current operating point at each iteration using a first-order Taylor expansion, then solving the resulting linear system to obtain a correction vector. Mathematically:
\begin{equation}
F(x^{(k)}) + J^{(k)} \Delta x^{(k)} = 0 \quad \implies \quad \Delta x^{(k)} = -\left[J^{(k)}\right]^{-1} F(x^{(k)})
\label{eq:NR_update}
\end{equation}
where $x = [\delta_2, \ldots, \delta_N, |V_{PQ}|]^T$ is the state vector (excluding the slack bus angle and PV bus voltage magnitudes, which are specified), and $F(x) = [\Delta P, \Delta Q]^T$ is the mismatch vector.

The matrix $J$ is the Jacobian --- it contains all the partial derivatives of the mismatch equations with respect to the state variables. It has the block structure:
\begin{equation}
J = \begin{bmatrix}
J_1 & J_2 \\
J_3 & J_4
\end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial \Delta P}{\partial \delta} & \dfrac{\partial \Delta P}{\partial |V|} \\[8pt]
\dfrac{\partial \Delta Q}{\partial \delta} & \dfrac{\partial \Delta Q}{\partial |V|}
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:jacobian_structure}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Jacobian Element Derivation}
The analytical derivation of each of the four Jacobian sub-matrices is presented below. All derivatives follow from Equations~(\ref{eq:P_calc}) and~(\ref{eq:Q_calc}). The derivation follows the procedure in Saadat~\cite{saadat} (Chapter~6) and Grainger~\cite{grainger} (Chapter~9).

\subsubsection{Sub-matrix \texorpdfstring{$J_1$: $\partial P_i / \partial \delta_j$}{J1: dPi/ddeltaj}}

\textbf{Off-diagonal elements ($i \neq j$):}

The only term in Equation~(\ref{eq:P_calc}) that depends on $\delta_j$ is the $k = j$ term:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial \delta_j} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial \delta_j} \left[ |V_i| |V_j| |Y_{ij}| \cos(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \right] \nonumber \\
&= |V_i| |V_j| |Y_{ij}| \cdot \left[ -\sin(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \right] \cdot (+1) \nonumber \\
&= -|V_i| |V_j| |Y_{ij}| \sin(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \label{eq:J1_offdiag}
\end{align}
The $(+1)$ comes from the chain rule: $\partial(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j)/\partial \delta_j = +1$.

\textbf{Diagonal elements ($i = j$):}

When $i = j$, $\delta_i$ appears in every term with coefficient $-1$. Differentiating:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial \delta_i} = \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq i}}^{N} |V_i| |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \sin(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:J1_diag}
\end{equation}
The sign is positive because: $\partial/\partial\delta_i$ of $\cos(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)$ gives $(-1)\times(-\sin(\cdot)) = +\sin(\cdot)$. The $k = i$ term vanishes because the diagonal Y-bus element $Y_{ii}$ appears in the expression as $|V_i|^2 |Y_{ii}| \cos(\theta_{ii} - \delta_i + \delta_i) = |V_i|^2 |Y_{ii}| \cos(\theta_{ii})$, which is independent of $\delta_i$. Therefore $\partial/\partial\delta_i \left[ |V_i|^2 |Y_{ii}| \cos(\theta_{ii}) \right] = 0$.

\subsubsection{Sub-matrix \texorpdfstring{$J_2$: $\partial P_i / \partial |V_j|$}{J2: dPi/d|Vj|}}

\textbf{Off-diagonal elements ($i \neq j$):}

Only the $k = j$ term in Equation~(\ref{eq:P_calc}) contains $|V_j|$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial |V_j|} &= |V_i| |Y_{ij}| \cos(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \label{eq:J2_offdiag}
\end{align}

\textbf{Diagonal elements ($i = j$):}

$|V_i|$ appears in every term. For the self-term ($k = i$), the product is $|V_i|^2 |Y_{ii}|\cos\theta_{ii}$, giving a derivative of $2|V_i||Y_{ii}|\cos\theta_{ii}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial P_i}{\partial |V_i|} = 2|V_i| |Y_{ii}| \cos\theta_{ii} + \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq i}}^{N} |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \cos(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:J2_diag}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Sub-matrix \texorpdfstring{$J_3$: $\partial Q_i / \partial \delta_j$}{J3: dQi/ddeltaj}}

\textbf{Off-diagonal elements ($i \neq j$):}

Differentiating Equation~(\ref{eq:Q_calc}) with respect to $\delta_j$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial \delta_j} &= -|V_i| |V_j| |Y_{ij}| \cos(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \label{eq:J3_offdiag}
\end{align}

\textbf{Diagonal elements ($i = j$):}

Applying the same chain rule logic as $J_1$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial \delta_i} = \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq i}}^{N} |V_i| |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \cos(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:J3_diag}
\end{equation}
The $k = i$ term drops out because $\sin(\theta_{ii})$ is constant with respect to $\delta_i$.

\subsubsection{Sub-matrix \texorpdfstring{$J_4$: $\partial Q_i / \partial |V_j|$}{J4: dQi/d|Vj|}}

\textbf{Off-diagonal elements ($i \neq j$):}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial |V_j|} = -|V_i| |Y_{ij}| \sin(\theta_{ij} - \delta_i + \delta_j) \label{eq:J4_offdiag}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Diagonal elements ($i = j$):}

The $k = i$ self-term contributes $-2|V_i||Y_{ii}|\sin\theta_{ii}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial |V_i|} = -2|V_i| |Y_{ii}| \sin\theta_{ii} - \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k \neq i}}^{N} |V_k| |Y_{ik}| \sin(\theta_{ik} - \delta_i + \delta_k)
\label{eq:J4_diag}
\end{equation}

The diagonal of $J_4$ is particularly important: it directly measures how sensitive the reactive power balance at bus~$i$ is to changes in $|V_i|$. This quantity is exploited in the sensitivity analysis (Section~5). All four sub-matrices are recomputed at every iteration to maintain quadratic convergence~\cite{tinney1967}.

\subsection{Newton-Raphson Over Other Methods}

The selection of Newton-Raphson over alternative iterative methods requires analytical justification. This subsection presents the reasoning behind this choice.

\subsubsection{Comparison with Gauss-Seidel}
The Gauss-Seidel (GS) method is simpler to implement --- the power balance equation is rearranged to isolate $V_i$ and the solution is obtained by successive substitution. However, GS exhibits a fundamental limitation: it converges \textit{linearly}. The error at each iteration is approximately proportional to the previous error:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{k+1} \approx C \cdot \epsilon_k \qquad \text{(linear convergence)}
\end{equation}
For the IEEE 9-bus system, PSS/E's Gauss-Seidel solver required 18 iterations to converge, whereas the developed Newton-Raphson solver converged in 4. This disparity becomes more pronounced for larger systems --- for a 300-bus network, GS may require hundreds of iterations while NR typically converges in 5--6.

\subsubsection{Comparison with Fast Decoupled Load Flow}
The Fast Decoupled method (FDLF) exploits the physical coupling structure in transmission systems: $P$ is predominantly coupled to $\delta$ and $Q$ to $|V|$. By neglecting the off-diagonal sub-matrices $J_2$ and $J_3$, each iteration is rendered computationally cheaper, requiring only two smaller linear system solves instead of one large one.

However, FDLF relies on the assumption that $X \gg R$ (the line reactance is much larger than the resistance). While this is generally true for high-voltage transmission lines, it breaks down for distribution systems or systems with unusual topology. For the IEEE 9-bus system, FDLF converged in 3 iterations --- comparable to full NR --- but the voltage magnitudes showed slightly less precision in the last decimal place.

\subsubsection{Justification for Newton-Raphson}
The Newton-Raphson method was selected for the following reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It exhibits \textbf{quadratic convergence}: $\epsilon_{k+1} \propto \epsilon_k^2$. Once the error becomes small, it reduces to machine precision within one additional iteration. This behaviour is observed in the solver's convergence from a mismatch of 0.187~pu at Iteration~2 to $3.4 \times 10^{-7}$~pu at Iteration~4.
    \item It is \textbf{robust}: unlike FDLF, it does not rely on assumptions regarding the network's R/X ratio.
    \item The Jacobian provides additional utility: the diagonal elements of $J_4$ (i.e., $\partial Q / \partial |V|$) directly yield the voltage sensitivity $dV/dQ$, which is exploited in the sensitivity analysis (Section~5).
    \item It demonstrates adequate \textbf{tolerance to initial conditions}. With a flat start ($|V|=1.0$, $\delta=0$), NR converges reliably for well-conditioned systems. No divergence was encountered for this problem; failure modes are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:convergence_discussion}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Convergence Properties and Failure Modes}
\label{sec:convergence_discussion}
While the Newton-Raphson method converges robustly for this problem, understanding its failure modes is essential for general applicability.

\subsubsection{Ill-Conditioned Jacobian}
As a system approaches its voltage collapse point (the ``nose'' of the P--V curve), the Jacobian becomes increasingly ill-conditioned. Physically, this corresponds to the reactive power demand at a bus nearing the maximum that the network can supply. The determinant of $J$ approaches zero, causing the correction vector $\Delta x = J^{-1} \cdot \Delta S$ to grow without bound~\cite{saadat}. In practice, the solver will either produce oscillating voltage estimates or generate corrections that push voltages to physically unreasonable values (e.g., $|V| < 0$). For the IEEE 9-bus system at nominal loading, the Jacobian is well-conditioned and far from collapse.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to Initial Guess}
The basin of convergence for Newton-Raphson is finite. A flat start ($|V| = 1.0$~pu, $\delta = 0^{\circ}$) works reliably for well-conditioned transmission systems because the solution is typically close to this initial point. However, for heavily loaded systems or distribution networks with depressed voltages, a flat start may place the initial estimate outside the convergence basin. In such cases, continuation methods or graduated loading strategies may be required~\cite{grainger}.

\subsubsection{PV Bus Reactive Power Limits}
PV buses maintain a specified voltage magnitude by adjusting their reactive power output. Real generators have finite reactive capability ($Q_{min} \leq Q_G \leq Q_{max}$). If a PV bus exceeds its reactive limit during iteration, it must be reclassified as a PQ bus (with $Q$ fixed at its limit), and the voltage is allowed to deviate from the setpoint. This bus type switching can cause convergence difficulties. The developed implementation does not include this logic, as the IEEE 9-bus system does not violate reactive limits under nominal loading.

\section{Newton-Raphson Implementation}

\subsection{Program Structure}
The entire solver is contained in a single Python file (\texttt{E21291\_LoadFlow.py}), as required by the submission guidelines. The program is structured into the following functions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{get\_ieee\_9\_bus\_data()} --- Returns all network data: bus types, specified powers, initial voltages, and branch impedances.
    \item \texttt{build\_y\_bus()} --- Constructs the $N \times N$ complex admittance matrix from branch data. Off-nominal transformer tap ratios are handled by modifying the $\pi$-equivalent circuit model.
    \item \texttt{newton\_raphson()} --- The main iterative solver. At each iteration, it computes the power mismatches, checks convergence, assembles the full Jacobian, solves the linear system using NumPy's \texttt{linalg.solve}, and updates the voltage state vector.
    \item \texttt{calculate\_line\_flows()} --- Post-convergence, computes the MW and MVAr flow on each branch, and accumulates system losses.
    \item \texttt{print\_results()} and \texttt{save\_results\_to\_csv()} --- Output formatting and data export.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Bus Type Handling}
The IEEE 9-bus system has three types of buses, and each type contributes differently to the mismatch equations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slack Bus (Bus 1):} Both $|V|$ and $\delta$ are fixed. It absorbs whatever generation is needed to balance the system. No mismatch equations are written for this bus.
    \item \textbf{PV Buses (Buses 2, 3):} $P$ and $|V|$ are specified. Only a $\Delta P$ mismatch equation is written; voltage magnitude is not updated.
    \item \textbf{PQ Buses (Buses 4--9):} Both $P$ and $Q$ are specified. Both $\Delta P$ and $\Delta Q$ mismatches are included, and both $\delta$ and $|V|$ are updated.
\end{itemize}
Consequently, the Jacobian for the 9-bus system is a $14 \times 14$ matrix (8 $\Delta P$ equations for buses 2--9, plus 6 $\Delta Q$ equations for PQ buses 4--9).

\subsection{Algorithm Flow}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialise:} Load network data and set all voltages to a ``flat start'' ($|V| = 1.0$ pu, $\delta = 0^{\circ}$).
    \item \textbf{Build $Y_{bus}$:} Assemble the admittance matrix from line and transformer data.
    \item \textbf{Iteration loop (repeat until converged or max iterations reached):}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute $P_{calc}$ and $Q_{calc}$ at each bus using Equations~(\ref{eq:P_calc}) and (\ref{eq:Q_calc}).
        \item Form the mismatch vector $[\Delta P, \Delta Q]^T$.
        \item Check convergence: is $\max(|\Delta P|, |\Delta Q|) < \epsilon$?
        \item If not converged, build the Jacobian $J$ using the analytical derivatives from Section~2.4.
        \item Solve $J \cdot [\Delta \delta, \Delta |V|]^T = [\Delta P, \Delta Q]^T$.
        \item Update: $\delta \leftarrow \delta + \Delta \delta$ and $|V| \leftarrow |V| + \Delta |V|$.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Post-process:} Calculate line flows and system losses.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Manual Calculation Verification}
To verify correct implementation, two key intermediate calculations were checked by hand.

\subsubsection{Y-Bus Element Verification (Line 4--5)}
Line 4--5 has impedance $Z_{45} = 0.01 + j0.085$~pu and half-line charging susceptance $B/2 = 0.088$~pu. The off-diagonal admittance element is:
\begin{equation}
Y_{45} = -\frac{1}{Z_{45}} = -\frac{1}{0.01 + j0.085} = -\frac{0.01 - j0.085}{0.01^2 + 0.085^2} \approx -1.365 + j11.60 \text{~pu}
\end{equation}
This value was compared against the corresponding element $Y_{bus}[3,4]$ (zero-indexed) printed by the program, confirming an exact match.

\subsubsection{Initial Mismatch at Bus 5}
Bus 5 is a load bus with $P_{load} = 125$~MW $= 1.25$~pu and $Q_{load} = 50$~MVAr $= 0.50$~pu. In the sign convention used here (load is negative injection), $P_{spec,5} = -1.25$~pu.

At the flat start ($V_i = 1.0 \angle 0^{\circ}$ for all $i$), the calculated power injection at Bus~5 can be evaluated rigorously. Substituting $|V_i| = |V_k| = 1.0$ and $\delta_i = \delta_k = 0$ into Equation~(\ref{eq:P_calc}):
\begin{equation}
P_{5}^{calc,(0)} = \sum_{k=1}^{9} |Y_{5k}| \cos(\theta_{5k}) = \text{Re}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{9} Y_{5k}\right) = G_{55} + \sum_{k \neq 5} G_{5k}
\end{equation}
where $G$ denotes the real part of the admittance. For Bus~5, the Y-bus row sum evaluates to approximately $0.0$~pu (since the off-diagonal entries sum to $-Y_{55}$ for a lossless network, and the small resistive terms contribute negligibly). Therefore:
\begin{equation}
\Delta P_5^{(0)} = P_{spec,5} - P_{calc,5} = -1.25 - 0 = -1.25 \text{~pu}
\end{equation}
The program reported an initial maximum mismatch of 1.63~pu, occurring at Bus 7 (due to the large generator injection there). Bus 5's mismatch of $-1.25$~pu is consistent with this value.

\section{Voltage Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The load flow solution describes the system state under a specific loading condition. However, in practice, loads vary continuously. The more critical engineering question is: \textit{how sensitive are bus voltages to load perturbations?} And more specifically: \textit{which buses exhibit the greatest vulnerability?}

To address these questions, a parametric study was conducted in which active and reactive loads were varied independently by $\pm 10\%$, and the load flow was re-solved for each case to quantify the resulting voltage profile shifts.

\subsection{Methodology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base Case:} Nominal IEEE 9-bus loading (125~MW + j50~MVAr at Bus~5, 90~MW + j30~MVAr at Bus~6, 100~MW + j35~MVAr at Bus~8).
    \item \textbf{Independent Variation:} For each of the three load buses (5, 6, 8), its $P$ (or $Q$) was independently varied by $-10\%$, $0\%$, and $+10\%$ while all other loads remained at their nominal values.

\subsection{Discussion}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Basis of Q--V Coupling Dominance}
The observed dominance of reactive power over active power in determining voltage magnitudes is a fundamental consequence of transmission line impedance characteristics.

For a short transmission line, the voltage drop between the sending and receiving ends can be approximated as:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V \approx \frac{RP + XQ}{|V|}
\label{eq:voltage_drop}
\end{equation}
where $R$ and $X$ are the line resistance and reactance, and $P$ and $Q$ are the active and reactive power flows. In high-voltage transmission systems, the reactance is much larger than the resistance. For example, Line 4--5 in the IEEE 9-bus system has $R = 0.01$~pu and $X = 0.085$~pu --- an X/R ratio of 8.5. This means:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V \approx \frac{XQ}{|V|} \quad \text{since } X \gg R
\label{eq:voltage_drop_approx}
\end{equation}
So the voltage drop is \textit{almost entirely} determined by the reactive power flow. Changing $P$ has a comparatively small effect because it is multiplied by $R$, which is small. This is not just a mathematical artefact --- it reflects a fundamental physical property of inductance-dominated power systems.

Quantitatively, a 10\% increase in $Q$ at Bus 5 produced a voltage depression of $0.9956 - 0.9896 = 0.006$~pu, whereas a 10\% increase in $P$ yielded only $0.9956 - 0.9946 = 0.001$~pu. The ratio of these effects ($\approx 6:1$) is consistent with the X/R ratio of the connecting lines ($\approx 8.5$), providing empirical validation of Equation~(\ref{eq:voltage_drop_approx}).

\subsubsection{Identifying the Weak Bus}
\textbf{Bus 5 is the weakest bus in the system.} This conclusion is supported by three independent lines of evidence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lowest base-case voltage:} Even in the nominal case, Bus 5 has the lowest voltage (0.9956~pu). It is already operating below 1.0~pu, which means it has less ``headroom'' before hitting voltage limits.
    
    \item \textbf{Highest voltage sensitivity:} Under reactive power variations, Bus 5 shows a standard deviation of $28.5 \times 10^{-4}$ --- almost double that of Bus 6 ($16.2 \times 10^{-4}$) and more than double that of Bus 8 ($12.8 \times 10^{-4}$).
    
    \item \textbf{Large electrical distance from sources:} Bus 5 is connected to generators only through long transmission paths. The Thevenin impedance ``seen'' from Bus 5 looking back towards the slack bus is high, which means any additional reactive power demand at Bus 5 must travel through high-impedance paths, causing larger voltage drops. In contrast, Bus 7 (which is directly connected to the large generator at Bus 2) barely changes voltage at all.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Voltage Sensitivity Coefficients}
The diagonal elements of $J_4^{-1}$ at the converged solution provide the voltage sensitivity coefficients $\partial |V_i| / \partial Q_i$ (in pu/pu). These coefficients are computed as $-\text{diag}(J_4^{-1})$, where $J_4$ is evaluated at the converged operating point. Since $J_4$ represents $\partial Q / \partial |V|$, its inverse diagonal elements give $\partial |V| / \partial Q$. These are more physically intuitive than the standard-deviation-based metrics above, as they directly indicate how much the voltage at a bus will change per unit of reactive power injection. The values extracted from the final Jacobian are:

\subsubsection{Operational Recommendations}
\label{sec:operational_recommendations}
The analysis identifies Bus~5 as the voltage-critical bus in the system. Several operational insights follow:

\textbf{(a) Voltage Margin Assessment.}
Under nominal loading, Bus~5 operates at 0.9956~pu. The IEEE standard operating voltage band for transmission systems is typically $\pm 5\%$ (i.e., 0.95 to 1.05~pu)~\cite{ieee1547}. Bus~5 has only 4.56\% margin before the lower voltage limit (0.95~pu) is violated, compared to 5.59\% for Bus~6 at 1.0127~pu. The observed 1.2\% voltage swing under $\pm 10\%$ reactive power variation is therefore acceptable under normal conditions, but the limited margin at Bus~5 leaves little room for contingency events.

\textbf{(b) Reactive Power Compensation.}
A shunt capacitor at Bus~5 would be the most effective voltage support measure. Using the sensitivity coefficient ($\partial |V_5|/\partial Q_5 \approx 0.057$~pu/pu), raising Bus~5's voltage to 1.0~pu would require approximately:
\begin{equation}
\Delta Q_{cap} \approx \frac{1.0 - 0.9956}{0.057} \approx 0.077 \text{~pu} = 7.7 \text{~MVAr}
\end{equation}
This is a modest capacitor bank that could be readily accommodated at a 230~kV substation. This calculation assumes the sensitivity remains approximately constant for small perturbations, which is valid within $\pm 0.01$~pu of the operating point~\cite{saadat}.

\textbf{(c) N-1 Contingency Considerations.}
Loss of the line connecting Bus~4 to Bus~5 would isolate Bus~5 from the slack bus path, significantly increasing its Thevenin impedance and worsening voltage regulation. Similarly, loss of the generator at Bus~3 (which supplies reactive power to the southeastern portion of the network) would further depress Bus~5's voltage. A comprehensive contingency analysis is beyond the scope of this assignment but is recommended for a complete operational assessment~\cite{anderson}.

\textbf{(d) Load Model Assumption.}
The preceding analysis assumes constant power loads, where $P$ and $Q$ are independent of voltage. In practice, loads exhibit voltage dependence (e.g., constant impedance or composite ZIP models), which would partially self-regulate: as voltage drops, load decreases, partially mitigating the voltage decline. The sensitivity coefficients reported above therefore represent a conservative (worst-case) estimate~\cite{saadat}.

\section{Conclusion}

This project developed a full Newton-Raphson load flow solver from first principles and applied it to the IEEE 9-bus test system~\cite{anderson}. The principal contribution lies not only in obtaining numerically correct results, but in establishing a transparent, mathematically justified framework that connects algorithmic behaviour to physical system properties.

The Newton-Raphson method converged in 4 iterations, reducing the power mismatch from 1.63~pu to $3.4 \times 10^{-7}$~pu. Quadratic convergence was clearly exhibited in the iteration history --- each step reduced the error by approximately the square of the previous error. This represents a significant practical advantage over Gauss-Seidel, which required 18 iterations for the same problem~\cite{saadat}. The additional computational cost per iteration (Jacobian assembly and factorisation) is justified by the substantially faster convergence.

Systematic comparison with PSS/E confirmed the correctness of the implementation. Voltage magnitudes matched to all four decimal places (RMSE = 0.0000~pu), and angles differed by at most $0.005^{\circ}$. Total active power losses also agreed precisely (4.64~MW vs 4.6~MW from PSS/E). These results verify the correctness of the Y-bus construction, Jacobian formulation, and mismatch equations.

The sensitivity analysis identified Bus~5 as the weakest bus in the system, with the highest voltage sensitivity coefficient ($\partial |V|/\partial Q \approx 0.057$~pu/pu) and the lowest base-case voltage (0.9956~pu). The dominance of reactive power over active power in determining voltage magnitudes --- with a sensitivity ratio consistent with line X/R ratios --- was both theoretically justified and empirically demonstrated. From an operational perspective, a 7.7~MVAr shunt capacitor at Bus~5 would restore its voltage to 1.0~pu, and the limited 4.56\% margin to the IEEE lower voltage limit underscores the need for reactive power management at this bus.
